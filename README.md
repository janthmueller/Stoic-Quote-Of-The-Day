If the Divine is faithful, he also must be faithful; if free, he also must be free; if beneficent, he also must be beneficent; if magnanimous, he also must be magnanimous. Thus as an imitator of God must he follow Him in every deed and word.
<!--END_SECTION:quote-text--></em><br>
    <strong>— <!--START_SECTION:quote-author-->
Epictetus
<!--END_SECTION:quote-author--></strong>
</p>

<div align="center" style="max-width:600px;margin:0 auto;">
<!--START_SECTION:quote-interpretation-->
Drawing inspiration from the divine qualities that Epictetus highlights, the passage encourages us to emulate traits such as faithfulness, freedom, beneficence, and magnanimity in our daily lives. In today’s world, this could mean striving to be as reliable and trustworthy as we wish those around us to be—whether at work, with friends, or in our families. To be free is to live authentically and courageously, making choices that align with our true values rather than succumbing to societal pressures or fleeting desires. Beneficence might manifest in acts of kindness and generosity, whereby we contribute to our community and support those in need, not for recognition, but as a natural expression of goodwill. Magnanimity involves rising above pettiness and minor grievances; it is the art of maintaining a big heart even when faced with life's frustrations and challenges. In practicing these virtues, we mirror the steadfast wisdom and strength that the Stoics believed reflected divine nature. By aspiring to embody these qualities, whether through small acts of kindness or significant life choices, we maintain a consistency between our ideals and actions, creating harmony within ourselves and with the world around us.
